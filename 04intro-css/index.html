Exposure Fundamentals

Exposure is one of the most important ideas in photography. It describes how bright or dark a photograph appears, based on the amount of light that reaches the camera's sensor. A correctly exposed image usually contains useful detail in both bright and dark areas. However, “correct” exposure is also creative: a photographer may intentionally make an image darker for mood or brighter for an airy, high-key look.

To control exposure, photographers use three main camera settings:

  • Aperture
  • Shutter speed
  • ISO

Together, these are known as the exposure triangle. Each setting affects the brightness of the image, but each also changes another visual quality. Learning to balance those effects is the foundation of confident camera use.

What Happens When You Take a Photograph?

When you press the shutter button, light travels through the lens and reaches the camera sensor. The camera must decide, or you must choose, three things:

How wide should the lens opening be?
This is controlled by the aperture.
How long should light reach the sensor?
This is controlled by the shutter speed.
How sensitive should the sensor be to light?
This is controlled by the ISO.

The Exposure Triangle

There are three elements that work together to properly expose an image.

Aperture: Controlling the Size of the Lens Opening

The aperture is an adjustable opening inside a lens. Like the pupil of an eye, it can widen in low light or become smaller in bright conditions.

A small f-number, such as f/1.8, means a wide aperture opening. A large f-number, such as f/16, means a small aperture opening.

  • f/2.8
  • f/4
  • f/5.6
  • f/8
  • f/11
  • f/16
  • f/22

Shutter Speed: Controlling Time and Movement

The shutter is like a curtain that opens and closes in front of the sensor. Shutter speed controls how long the shutter stays open. It is usually measured in seconds or fractions of a second:

A fast shutter speed, such as 1/1000 second, lets in light for a very short time. A slower shutter speed, such as 1/30 second or one full second, lets in light for longer. Only the bottom of the fraction is displayed on the camera display so just remember that these are fractions.

  • 30
  • 60
  • 125
  • 250
  • 500
  • 1000

ISO: Controlling Sensor Sensitivity

ISO controls how strongly the camera sensor responds to the light it receives. A lower ISO number, such as ISO 100, is less sensitive to light. A higher number, such as ISO 3200, is more sensitive. Common ISO settings include:

  • ISO 200
  • ISO 400
  • ISO 800
  • ISO 1600
  • ISO 3200
